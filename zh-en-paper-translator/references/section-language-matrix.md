# Section-Specific Language Matrix

Choose tense, voice, vocabulary, and sentence shape by the sentence's rhetorical job. The entries below are defaults, not mechanical templates.

| Section | Main job | Typical tense | Voice and sentence strategy | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Title | Name the study object and contribution precisely | Usually tenseless; present tense when a full clause is necessary | Prefer compact, information-bearing syntax; retain the source scope | Adding novelty or causal force |
| Abstract | Compress context, objective, approach, key result, and bounded implication | Present for established context and implications; past for work performed and results observed | Mix active and passive according to focus; keep agents explicit when they matter | Turning a compact summary into an overclaim |
| Introduction | Establish knowledge, limits, gap, and study objective | Present for accepted knowledge/current gap; present perfect for an active research trajectory; past for specific prior studies | Use clear subjects and logical transitions; avoid literal topic-comment chains | Inventing a gap or contribution absent from the source |
| Methods | Make procedures and analysis reproducible | Past for completed study procedures; present for equations, definitions, and generally valid operations | Passive is acceptable when the procedure is the focus; active is better when the actor prevents ambiguity | Hiding missing conditions behind polished prose |
| Results | Report observations and quantitative comparisons | Predominantly past; present may describe what a figure/table shows | Put the result or measured object in subject position; use active or passive according to information focus | Adding interpretation, mechanism, or causality |
| Discussion | Interpret findings, compare literature, explain limits, and state implications | Past for this study's observations; present for interpretation/current knowledge; modals for bounded possibilities | Use calibrated hedging and explicit comparison; preserve rival explanations and limitations | Upgrading `consistent with` to `demonstrates` |
| Conclusion | Restate contribution, evidence, implication, and boundary | Past for completed work; present for supported conclusion; modals for prospective implications | Prefer direct synthesis; keep the implication no broader than the evidence | Introducing new data or universal claims |
| Caption | Identify content and explain encodings or conditions | Present for what the figure/table shows; past for study actions where needed | Use fragments only when conventional and unambiguous | Losing panel labels, units, or sample definitions |

## Voice Decisions

Do not equate academic English with passive voice.

- Use active voice when the actor matters or when passive syntax hides responsibility: `We trained the model...`.
- Use passive voice when the operation or object is the information focus and the actor is obvious or irrelevant: `Samples were stored at ...`.
- Do not introduce `we` if the Chinese source, manuscript convention, or authorship context does not support it. Recast with the study, analysis, method, or measured object as subject.
- Avoid long passive chains with empty subjects, especially literal translations of “对……进行了……”.

## Claim-Strength Ladder

Preserve the source level; do not move upward without evidence:

`is consistent with / may indicate < suggests < supports < shows / demonstrates < establishes / proves`

These terms are not interchangeable. Causal verbs such as `drives`, `leads to`, and `results in` require causal support in the source and study design.

## Chinese-to-English Reconstruction

- Replace topic-comment chains with explicit subject-verb relations.
- Split comma chains when they contain independent propositions.
- Merge repetitive short sentences only when no condition or emphasis is lost.
- Restore a connector only when the relation is supported by context; otherwise flag the ambiguity.
- Keep negation, exception, comparison direction, time order, and scope visible.
- Avoid translating formulaic Chinese phrases into inflated expressions such as `has important guiding significance` unless the source specifies the concrete significance.
